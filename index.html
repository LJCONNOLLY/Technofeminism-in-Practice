Feminist Theory Meets Technology Practice

This graduate seminar examines the gendered and racialized assumptions embedded in digital infrastructures. We will analyze several technological infrastructures including search algorithms and interface design, and then conclude the class by asking what it would take to build something different. We'll read across feminist science and technology studies, critical data studies, and design justice, tracing a throughline from Haraway's situated knowledges to the present moment's AI tools. Each week pairs a theoretical framework with a concrete case.

About This Course

Course Description

The course is organized around a single signature project with two tracks. In the Critical Platform Audit track, you'll conduct a theoretical audit of an existing platform or algorithm. In the Feminist Platform Design track, you'll build a small working prototype using Claude Code by arguing for specific design choices as answers to specific critiques from our reading. Both tracks ask the same underlying question: given what we know about how technology encodes bias, what would it mean to do otherwise? Generative AI tools are part of what we study this semester, not just how we work, and you'll be asked to reflect critically on your own use of them regardless of which track you choose.

Course Objectives

By the end of this semester, students will be able to:

  • Analyze how digital platforms and algorithms encode gendered and racialized assumptions, using frameworks from feminist STS and critical data studies (Haraway, Noble, Benjamin, Eubanks, and others).
  • Evaluate a scholar's argument against at least one other framework from the course to construct an original claim about the feminism-technology relationship, distinct from a summary.
  • Apply design justice principles to critique or construct a digital interface, tracing individual design decisions back to specific theoretical commitments.
  • Construct a functional prototype (Track B) or a sustained written audit (Track A) that demonstrates use of course concepts applied to a chosen case.
  • Assess the capabilities and limitations of a generative AI tool through direct, documented use, and articulate how that experience does or doesn't confirm the semester's critiques.
  • Articulate your evolving personal position on feminism and technology, making your own intellectual labor and changes of mind visible.

Signature Project: Two Tracks, One Question

Given what we know about how technology encodes bias, what would it mean to do otherwise? Both tracks answer that question—one through critique, one through construction.

Track A · Critical Platform Audit Track B · Feminist Platform Design

Track A: Critical Platform Audit

Conduct a theoretical audit of an existing platform or algorithm, applying course frameworks (Haraway, Noble, Benjamin, Eubanks, and others) to build an original, defensible argument—not a survey of the readings. Using AI tools anywhere in your process is optional; if you use one, you disclose it narratively in your reflection document. "I didn't use AI" is a complete and legitimate answer.

Track B: Feminist Platform Design

Build a small working prototype using Claude Code, arguing for specific design choices as answers to specific critiques from our reading. Using Claude Code is required, since building the prototype is the point of the track. Your design document must include a substantive narrative reflection on what Claude Code did and didn't do well, and where it revealed an assumption baked into the tool that you had to design around.

AI Policy

This course treats AI tools as objects of critical inquiry, not only as instruments, in keeping with a semester spent asking how tools encode assumptions. The policy differs by track and by assignment type:

Track A (Critical Platform Audit)

Using AI tools anywhere in your process (research, drafting, editing) is optional. If you use one, you must disclose it in your reflection document. I'm not asking for prompt logs or screenshots; I want a narrative account of what you used it for, what it got right, what it got wrong or missed, and how that shaped your final work. "I didn't use AI" is a complete and legitimate answer.

Track B (Feminist Platform Design)

Using Claude Code is required, since building the prototype is the point of the track. Your design document must include a substantive narrative reflection addressing what you asked it to help build, where it was useful or unexpectedly good, where it failed or revealed an assumption baked into the tool that you had to design around, and whether the experience changed how you think about AI tools in relation to our readings.

Weekly Reading Responses & Seminar Participation

You may use AI tools to help you work through dense or unfamiliar theoretical language, but the response you submit needs to represent your own reading and thinking. A reading response that is substantively AI-generated doesn't fulfill the assignment, since the point of the exercise is your engagement with the text, not a summary of it.

Note on AI Opt-Out

Students who prefer not to use AI tools at all may do so freely on Track A and for weekly work. This is a deliberate feature of the policy, not an oversight; the Critical AI literacy rubric line is designed so that opting out is a complete and fully creditable answer on Track A.

Because norms around these tools continue to shift, I may revisit this policy over the semester in dialogue with the seminar.

Assignments & Grading

Your course grade is made up of four components:

30%

Weekly Reading Responses & Seminar Participation

A short written response (300–500 words) posted before each class session, plus active, prepared participation in seminar discussion.

15%

Signature Project Proposal (Week 11)

A short proposal identifying your chosen track, the platform you'll audit or the prototype you'll design, and your initial theoretical framing.

20%

Presentation & Peer Feedback (Week 14)

An in-class presentation of your project-in-progress and written peer feedback for two classmates.

35%

Final Signature Project (Track A or Track B)

Your Critical Platform Audit essay or Feminist Platform Design prototype and design document, plus a reflection document. Due Week 15.

Final Signature Project: Grading Rubric

Theoretical Engagement — 30%

Accurate, specific use of at least three course scholars/frameworks, applied precisely to your chosen case.

Argument & Personal Position — 35%

A clear, defensible argument that states your own position on the feminism-technology relationship, not a survey of the readings.

Critical AI Literacy — 20%

Track A: honest, specific disclosure if AI was used. Track B: genuine account of what Claude Code did and didn't do well.

Reflection Document — 15%

Your intellectual labor made visible: what you changed your mind about, and what the process taught you.

Late Policy

Reading responses are meant to prepare you for that week's discussion, so late responses (submitted after class starts) don't earn credit; life happens, and I'll drop your single lowest reading-response score at the end of the semester to account for it. The proposal and final project (including the reflection document) each carry a 48-hour grace period with no penalty and no questions asked; after that, work loses 3% per day late. If something bigger is going on, email me before the deadline where possible and we'll work out an extension.
