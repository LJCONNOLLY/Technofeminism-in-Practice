Learning Objectives

  • Understand Haraway's concept of the cyborg as a feminist figuration
  • Explore situated knowledges as an epistemological framework
  • Apply cyborg theory to contemporary digital technologies
  • Trace the evolution of feminist STS

Overview

Donna Haraway's work has been foundational to technofeminist thought. This week, we dive into two of her most influential texts: the Cyborg Manifesto (1985) and "Situated Knowledges" (1988).

The cyborg—a hybrid of machine and organism—becomes a potent metaphor for challenging binary thinking (human/machine, nature/culture, male/female) and imagining new forms of identity and politics in technological contexts.

📚 Readings

Haraway, Donna (1985). "A Cyborg Manifesto: Science, Technology, and Socialist-Feminism in the Late Twentieth Century."

Socialist Review, 80, 65-108.

This is a dense but rewarding text. Focus on: What is a cyborg for Haraway? What boundaries does it transgress? How might it be useful for feminist politics? Note the section on "women in the integrated circuit."

Access Reading

Haraway, Donna (1988). "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective."

Feminist Studies, 14(3), 575-599.

Pay attention to Haraway's critique of both relativism and objectivity. What does she mean by "situated" knowledge? How does this relate to accountability in science and technology?

Access Reading

Zobl, Elke & Drüeke, Ricarda (2021). "Feminist Technology."

Feminist Media: Participatory Spaces, Networks and Cultural Citizenship. Transcript Verlag, 93-110.

A contemporary application of Haraway's ideas to digital media and maker cultures. How are feminist makers using technology today?

Interactive Activity: Feminist Tech Timeline

Explore the Evolution of Feminist STS

Navigate through this interactive timeline of key moments, texts, and interventions in feminist science and technology studies.

1 / 6
1970s

Early Feminist Critiques of Science

Evelyn Fox Keller and Sandra Harding challenge the myth of scientific objectivity, arguing that knowledge is always situated and shaped by gender, power, and positionality.

Key Texts: Keller's "Gender and Science" (1978), Harding's "The Science Question in Feminism" (1986)

1985

Haraway's Cyborg Manifesto

Donna Haraway publishes "A Cyborg Manifesto," proposing the cyborg as a figuration that breaks down binaries between human/machine, nature/culture, and opens new political possibilities beyond identity politics.

Key Concept: Situated knowledges, partial perspectives, irony over innocence

1990s

Cyberfeminism Emerges

Artist collectives like VNS Matrix and theorists like Sadie Plant explore feminist futures in cyberspace. The internet is imagined as a space of possibility for gender fluidity and resistance to patriarchal structures.

Key Works: VNS Matrix's "Cyberfeminist Manifesto for the 21st Century" (1991), Plant's "Zeros + Ones" (1997)

2000s

Web 2.0 and Feminist Digital Labor Debates

The rise of social media platforms prompts feminist scholars to analyze the gendered dynamics of digital labor, affective labor, and platform capitalism. Questions about who benefits from user-generated content become central.

Key Themes: Immaterial labor, precarity, digital domesticity

2010s

Algorithmic Bias Becomes Visible

Scholars like Safiya Noble and Joy Buolamwini expose how algorithms perpetuate racism and sexism. The "black box" of AI is opened to reveal discriminatory training data and design choices.

Key Works: Noble's "Algorithms of Oppression" (2018), Buolamwini's "Gender Shades" project (2018)

2020s

AI Ethics, Design Justice, Data Feminism

Intersectional frameworks shape conversations about AI ethics, algorithmic accountability, and design justice. Scholars advocate for community-driven design, data sovereignty, and abolitionist approaches to technology.

Key Frameworks: Design Justice (Costanza-Chock), Data Feminism (D'Ignazio & Klein), Abolitionist tools

Podcast

Logic Magazine: "Technically Human" - Interview with Donna Haraway

Haraway reflects on the enduring relevance of cyborg feminism in the age of AI, climate crisis, and platform capitalism.

Assignment: Cyborg Analysis

Choose one contemporary technology and analyze it through Haraway's cyborg lens:

  • Prosthetics or assistive technologies
  • Wearable fitness trackers
  • Smartphone + body assemblage
  • Social media avatars/digital selves
  • Brain-computer interfaces

Questions to address:

  • What boundaries does this technology blur or transgress?
  • How might it reproduce or challenge existing power relations (gender, race, ability)?
  • What would a "situated knowledge" perspective reveal about this tech?

Format: 2-3 page written analysis OR 5-minute audio essay

Key Takeaways

  • The cyborg is a feminist figuration that refuses binary thinking
  • Situated knowledges provide accountability without claims to universal truth
  • Technology is never neutral—it's always entangled with power, labor, and embodiment
  • Feminist STS has a rich history that continues to evolve
