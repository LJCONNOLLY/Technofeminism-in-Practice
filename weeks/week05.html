Week 5

Algorithms of Oppression

Search engines, visibility, and racialized results

3 readings Interactive case study ~5 hours
← Week 4 All Modules Week 6 →

Learning Objectives

  • Understand how algorithms reproduce and amplify oppression
  • Analyze the political economy of search engines and recommendation systems
  • Identify racialized and gendered patterns in algorithmic outputs
  • Connect algorithmic bias to historical patterns of discrimination

Overview

Safiya Umoja Noble's groundbreaking work Algorithms of Oppression (2018) demonstrates how search engines are not neutral tools but rather reinforce and amplify existing racial and gender hierarchies. This week, we examine how algorithmic systems shape visibility, representation, and access to information.

Noble shows that Google's search results for queries like "black girls" once returned primarily pornographic content—a stark example of how algorithms can perpetuate harmful stereotypes. We'll explore the mechanisms behind such biases and their real-world consequences.

📚 Readings

Noble, Safiya Umoja (2018). Algorithms of Oppression: How Search Engines Reinforce Racism.

New York University Press. Read: Introduction + Chapters 1-2

Noble introduces the concept of "technological redlining" and shows how search algorithms systematically discriminate. Pay attention to her methodology—how does she document and analyze these patterns?

Access Reading

Gillespie, Tarleton (2014). "The Relevance of Algorithms."

In Tarleton Gillespie, Pablo J. Boczkowski, and Kirsten A. Foot (eds.), Media Technologies: Essays on Communication, Materiality, and Society. MIT Press, 167-194.

How do platforms construct "relevance"? What values and assumptions are encoded in ranking algorithms? Consider implications for whose voices are heard.

Access Reading

Noble, Safiya Umoja (2016). "A Future for Intersectional Black Feminist Technology Studies."

The Scholar and Feminist Online, 13.3-14.1.

Noble articulates a vision for Black feminist technology studies. What would it mean to center Black women's experiences in tech research and design?

Interactive Case Study: Search Engine Audit

Documenting Algorithmic Bias

Hands-On Activity

In this guided activity, you'll conduct your own mini-audit of search engine results to identify patterns of bias.

How to Conduct Your Audit
  1. Choose a search engine: Google, Bing, DuckDuckGo, etc.
  2. Use private/incognito mode to minimize personalization
  3. Document systematically: Take screenshots of first page results
  4. Note patterns: Who/what is visible? Who/what is absent? What narratives emerge?
  5. Compare across queries: How do results differ for gendered/racialized terms?

Ethical considerations: You may encounter disturbing content. Take care of yourself.

Suggested Search Queries to Compare

Compare results for parallel queries that differ by gender or race:

  • "professional hairstyles" vs. "professional Black hairstyles"
  • "CEO" image search vs. "female CEO"
  • "beautiful people" vs. "beautiful Asian people"
  • "artificial intelligence" vs. "artificial intelligence ethics"
  • "doctor" vs. "nurse" (check gender representation in images)

Or develop your own queries related to your interests/identities.

Analysis Framework

Use these questions to analyze your findings:

  • Representation: Who is visible/invisible? Stereotyped/humanized?
  • Source diversity: What types of websites appear? Whose voices are prioritized?
  • Commercial interests: How many results are ads or commercial sites?
  • Authority: What/who counts as an authoritative source?
  • Context collapse: Are distinct concepts conflated?
  • Historical patterns: How do results connect to longer histories of discrimination?

Assignment: Algorithmic Oppression Analysis

Complete your search engine audit (see interactive case study above) AND write a reflective analysis:

Your analysis should address:

  • What patterns of bias did you identify?
  • How do these patterns connect to Noble's concept of "algorithmic oppression"?
  • What are the material consequences of these biased results?
  • Who benefits from these algorithmic outputs? Who is harmed?
  • What interventions might address these issues? (Policy, design, regulation, etc.)

Format Options:

  • Written: 3-4 pages + screenshots/documentation
  • Video: 6-8 minute video essay with screen recordings
  • Zine: Visual/textual zine (digital or scanned) documenting findings

Key Takeaways

  • Algorithms are not neutral—they encode values, assumptions, and power relations
  • Search engines actively shape knowledge, visibility, and representation
  • Algorithmic bias is not a "glitch"—it's structural and requires structural solutions
  • Black feminist analysis reveals how race, gender, and capitalism intersect in algorithmic systems
