Week 5 · Sep 22

Algorithms of Oppression

Search, visibility, and racialized results.

Readings

Noble, Safiya Umoja. Algorithms of Oppression, Ch. 1 and Ch. 3.

Available via course reserves.

Reading Response Due

Come to class prepared with 5 ideas about the readings from the week that you want to discuss.

Example: for the introduction of Algorithms of Oppression, students could write something like:

  1. Noble uses "glitch" as the word companies hide behind, a one-off malfunction rather than the system working as designed. Later in the semester, Legacy Russell's Glitch Feminism reclaims that same word as a site of resistance and refusal. I want to ask the room whether these are actually opposed uses of "glitch," or whether Russell's reclamation only works because Noble is right that the system is stable enough to glitch against in the first place.
  2. Noble borrows Latoya Peterson's line that racism is "the fundamental API of the Internet," oppression running the same source code, just tweaked to context. That's a striking move: describing bias in the vocabulary of protocols and architecture rather than opinion or attitude. I want to test where that metaphor holds and where it might break down.
  3. Noble opens with Google engineers openly expressing sexist views while supposedly building "neutral" search infrastructure. I want to ask whether her argument is really about who builds the system, or something more structural, whether a different team under the same profit incentives would get a different result.
  4. Noble's core distinction: Google is an advertising company, not an information company, and that gets erased in how people actually trust it. I want to raise where else we see this collapse, treating commercial platform output as disinterested public information, and ask what an alternative infrastructure could even look like.
  5. "Technological redlining" and "algorithmic oppression" are doing a lot of theoretical work, and I want the room to nail down her definitions before we start applying them loosely. Then I want to test-drive them against something the book doesn't cover, hiring algorithms, predictive policing, or dating apps, to see if the concept actually travels.
